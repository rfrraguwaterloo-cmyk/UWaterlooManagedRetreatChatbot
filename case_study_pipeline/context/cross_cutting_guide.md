# RFR Cross-Cutting Themes Guide (Condensed)

Source: "RFR cross-cutting themes: A guide for starting to streamline research across cases" — Developed by Felicia Watterodt, with contributions from Brent Doberstein & Farah El-Shayeb, University of Waterloo, April 2025.

## Quick Reference

| Core Theme | Description |
|---|---|
| Socio-cultural Considerations | Explores the many social and cultural considerations that factor into local managed retreat decision-making processes and/or can impact community resiliency. |
| Institutional Environment | Explores the institutional environment that managed retreat decision-making processes are embedded within. |
| Community Engagement | Explores the manner and extent to which decision-makers engage the community in managed retreat planning processes. |
| Planning Processes | Explores community resiliency and climate adaptation planning processes, with managed retreat as one tool among a broader suite of options within the policy and planning toolkit. |

## Purpose

This guide provides all team members with a common starting point for engaging with research on the project. The four cross-cutting themes were identified through ongoing project-team discussions, the 2024 research design workshop, and the original proposal to the New Frontiers in Research Fund-International and the National Science Foundation.

The themes are intentionally broad, to be adapted to individual case study contexts. Examples in each section are a starting point, not an exhaustive list. The goal is a broad basis for comparative cross-case analysis across the project.

## Core Theme 1: Socio-Cultural Considerations

Socio-cultural contexts are inherently place-based, relating to the unique social structures, community dynamics, knowledge systems, and cultural norms, values, and worldviews situated within specific geographic, historical, and socio-political contexts. Incorporating social dynamics and community values into planning processes is crucial for proactive, equitable retreat.

Examples: place-based connections / place attachment; local, traditional, & Indigenous knowledge systems; multiple ways of knowing, doing, and being; lived experiences; community cohesion; collective effort and community mutual assistance; social capital; social acceptance; values and priorities; epistemology, ontology, spirituality.

## Core Theme 2: Institutional Environment

Institutions refer to the formal and informal ways in which society structures itself, determining the rules of the system and informing what actions are considered socially, politically, economically, and technically feasible. MR planning involves many institutional requirements (e.g., regulatory, legal, funding) and governance arrangements (e.g., jurisdictional authority) that can inhibit or incentivize proactive and equitable retreat planning.

Examples: funding mechanisms and requirements; legal and regulatory environment; enabling conditions and barriers; property/land ownership; land and water stewardship; Inherent Rights & Title / self-determination and self-governance; land acquisition; organizational/institutional capacity; zoning requirements, bylaws; jurisdictional authority / intergovernmental cooperation; good governance (transparent, accountable, evidence-based); informal, faith-based, and traditional institutions.

## Core Theme 3: Community Engagement

Climate change and adaptation planning affects many different groups (residents, local governments, First Nations, businesses, industries, infrastructure operators, marginalized populations) in a variety of ways. Inclusive and participatory processes are essential for proactive, equitable planning. Retreat is often socially and politically contentious and can lead to disproportionate impacts; voluntary, collaborative, community-led processes that centre local voices, values, and lived experiences are more likely to lead to equitable outcomes than top-down decision-making (e.g., forced relocation).

Examples: participatory processes; inclusive decision-making; co-design, co-development, co-governance; collaboration; whole-of-society; bridging silos; equity and justice; decolonization, reconciliation, Indigenization; Free, Prior, & Informed Consent; empowering grassroots/community organizations and local leadership; individual vs. collective participation; traditional engagement processes; representative decision-making/delegated authority.

## Core Theme 4: Planning Processes

Managed retreat can take a variety of forms (property buyout, managed realignment, land trusts) and can be implemented as a standalone strategy or as part of a broader community vision (post-retreat land use, phased adaptation, a mixed suite of options). Of particular interest is how MR can be implemented as a proactive adaptation strategy rather than in a reactive post-disaster recovery context, including multi-solving approaches that reduce flood risk while providing co-benefits to community and ecological well-being.

Examples: multi-solving / co-benefits / multi-benefits; adaptive, iterative planning; adaptation pathways; responding to shifting baselines and community dynamics over time; low-regrets solutions / avoiding maladaptation; multi-generational planning; holistic, systems thinking; more-than-human resilience; community resilience; built environment / critical infrastructure planning; life cycle planning / decommissioning infrastructure; gradual retreat / phased approaches; Protect, Accommodate, Retreat, Avoid (PARA); post-retreat land use planning; envisioning community futures; scenario planning; shoreline/watershed restoration.

## Note on Equitable, Proactive Managed Retreat

Managed retreat (MR) is an umbrella term for a group of flood adaptation strategies beyond reliance on protective infrastructure, including community resettlement, land acquisition, property buyouts, conservation easements, landowner lease-back programs, development setbacks, restrictions on rebuilding, gradual asset downzoning over time, removal of hard infrastructure, relocation of critical infrastructure, and ecosystem restoration. Strategic, participatory relocation can empower communities to re-envision sustainable development, engage in multi-generational disaster risk reduction strategies, reduce maladaptation, address existing inequities, and build on shared community values — though success is not guaranteed (a global study of 138 resettlement programs found 43% successful, 26% failed, 30% ongoing).

When coding cross-cutting themes, add notes for any of the four themes above that are relevant to a case but not captured by the main questionnaire categories.
